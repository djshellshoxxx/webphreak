Bluebox / MF / Trunk Signalling

In-band tones that telephone exchanges once used between themselves. Hold a key to sustain it. Keyboard: digits 0–9 play MF digits, K = KP, S = ST, X = 2600 Hz.

History: 2600 Hz, in-band signalling and the move out of band

In-band signalling

Mid-century long-distance trunks carried their control signals in the same audio channel as speech. Bell System engineers described SF and MF signalling in the Bell System Technical Journal, including the 1954 article on in-band single-frequency signalling (Nokia Bell Labs archive) and C. Breen & C. A. Dahlbom, “Signaling Systems for Control of Telephone Switching” (BSTJ, Nov. 1960; scan). Because the control tones travelled on the voice path, a caller who played them into the mouthpiece could be mistaken for exchange equipment; a “blue box” generated 2600 Hz and MF tones for that purpose (Wikipedia: 2600 hertz).

Out-of-band signalling

Common-channel signalling moved call setup onto a separate data network (CCIS in North America from the 1970s, later Signalling System No. 7), so speech-path tones no longer control trunks (Wikipedia: SS7). Digital transmission also displaced SF trunks. These pages are a listening archive; the tones have no effect on modern networks and nothing here connects to one.

Other systems

CCITT No. 4 and No. 5 served international routes; R2 MFC was widely used in Europe, Latin America and Asia with national variations (Wikipedia: R2). The UK used AC-family line signalling such as AC9 at 2280 Hz (britishtelephones.com).

Corrections made in this version: the old ST2P and ST3P keys reused the KP and ST pairs; they now use 1300 + 1700 Hz and 700 + 1700 Hz, consistent with the IANA telephone-event registry naming. Duplicate 2280 Hz keys were merged. See each key’s ⓘ panel.
