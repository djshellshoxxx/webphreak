History and technical help

2600 Hz and historical in-band signaling

Some mid-twentieth-century long-distance trunks carried supervisory signals in the same voice-band channel as speech. In certain North American single-frequency systems, 2600 Hz was associated with idle trunk supervision. Because voice and control shared the same path, those systems could be vulnerable to unintended control-tone detection. Later common-channel signaling, including SS7, moved call-control messages onto separate signaling networks; digital switching and out-of-band controls displaced these older arrangements. Not every historical exchange used identical signaling.

Red boxes and payphones

Red boxes imitated coin-deposit tones heard by older operator-assisted coin accounting systems. The signals shown here are historical audio demonstrations; their exact cadences and regional implementations varied. Later electronic payphones, including Nortel Millennium models, used more sophisticated electronic coin accounting and network interactions, making old coin-tone imitation techniques largely obsolete. Public payphones subsequently declined sharply as mobile phones became widespread; a small number still operate.

Other systems

British AC signaling used different trunk frequencies, while AUTOVON exposed the fourth DTMF column for military call precedence. Green-box terminology referred to historical coin collect/return control. Quick-Call II used sequential paging tones, and SIT sequences were sometimes imitated by consumer call-screening products. Historical claims about specific MF special codes can be network-dependent.
